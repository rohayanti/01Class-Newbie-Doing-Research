Module 1 · 30 min

Research Topic, Related Paper & Problem Formulation
🎯 Goal of Module 1

Read one existing paper, replicate it, find its weaknesses, and use those weaknesses to write your own research problem and objectives. This is how every real research project begins.

Step 1.1 — How to Choose a Research Topic

A good research topic sits at the intersection of three things: a dataset you can obtain, a method you can implement, and a gap — something the existing literature has not done well. Begin with a dataset you can access immediately. We will use the Iris dataset as our training ground.

A

Pick an accessible, well-studied dataset

Iris is ideal: 150 samples, 4 features, 3 classes, no missing values, built into scikit-learn. You fully understand its content in 10 minutes.

B

Search for 3–5 existing papers using that dataset

Use Google Scholar. Search: "iris dataset classification comparison". Read their abstracts. Pick one that is clear and replicable. That is your anchor paper.

C

Read carefully to find what they did not do

Every paper has limitations. Your job is to find one genuine gap — something they should have tested but did not. That gap becomes your research problem.

Step 1.2 — Your Anchor Paper (Sample)

Below is a representative example of the type of paper you will find. Study it exactly as described.

Lim, A., Hassan, M., & Yusof, N. (2022). A comparative study of supervised classifiers on the Fisher Iris dataset: Decision Tree, SVM, and k-Nearest Neighbour. Proceedings of the International Conference on Intelligent Systems and Computing, 14(1), 45–52. [Sample reference — for learning purposes]
DatasetIris (150 samples, 4 features, 3 classes)
Classifiers comparedDecision Tree, SVM (RBF kernel), k-NN (k=3)
Evaluation methodSingle 80/20 train–test split (random_state=1)
Metric reportedAccuracy only
Their reported resultsDT: 95.0% · SVM: 97.5% · k-NN: 96.7%
Feature analysisNone
Statistical significanceNot tested

Step 1.3 — Replicate the Paper's Solution

Before critiquing any paper, you must first reproduce it. If you cannot reproduce it, you cannot trust your criticism.

Python — Replicate the Anchor Paper
# Replicating Lim et al. (2022) — single split, accuracy only
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
from sklearn.svm import SVC
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score

iris = load_iris()
X, y = iris.data, iris.target

# Single 80/20 split — as described in the paper
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(
    X, y, test_size=0.2, random_state=1
)

for name, clf in [
    ('Decision Tree',  DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=42)),
    ('SVM (RBF)',       SVC(kernel='rbf', random_state=42)),
    ('k-NN (k=3)',      KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=3)),
]:
    clf.fit(X_tr, y_tr)
    acc = accuracy_score(y_te, clf.predict(X_te))
    print(f"{name:20} Accuracy: {acc:.1%}")

⚠️ Key observation

The winner changes when you change the random seed! k-NN goes from 96.7% to 100% just by splitting the data differently. The paper's conclusion ("SVM is best") cannot be trusted from a single split. This is your first finding.

Step 1.4 — Identify Weaknesses in the Paper

W1
Single train-test split is not robust. Changing the random seed changes the winner. Results depend on luck, not on genuine model performance.
W2
Only accuracy is reported. Accuracy can be misleading when class distributions are unequal. F1-score per class reveals whether any class is being misclassified consistently.
W3
No feature importance analysis. The paper does not tell us which of the four measurements (sepal/petal length/width) drives the classification. We do not know if all four features are necessary.
W4
No ablation study. Could we achieve the same accuracy with only 2 features instead of 4? Removing features one at a time (ablation) would answer this, but the paper never tried.
W5
No statistical significance test. Is SVM's 97.5% genuinely different from DT's 95.0%? Without a paired statistical test, we cannot claim SVM is superior.

Step 1.5 — Formulate Your Research Problem

Research Gap (derived from W1–W4)

Existing comparative studies on Iris classification use a single train-test split and report accuracy alone, which obscures model robustness across different data divisions. Furthermore, no study has systematically removed features one at a time to quantify the contribution of each floral measurement to classification accuracy — a critical step for understanding what the model actually learns.

Problem Statement

It is not known which combination of floral measurements in the Iris dataset is minimally sufficient for robust multi-class classification, nor whether reported classifier rankings hold across repeated cross-validation folds.

Step 1.6 — Write Your Research Objectives

RO1
To compare the classification performance of Decision Tree, Random Forest, and SVM on the Iris dataset using 10-fold stratified cross-validation and F1-Macro as the primary metric. [addresses W1, W2]
RO2
To conduct a feature ablation study — removing one feature at a time and testing all pairwise subsets — to quantify each measurement's contribution to classification performance. [addresses W3, W4]
RO3
To identify the minimal feature subset that maintains F1-Macro ≥ 0.95 across all three classifiers, providing evidence-based feature reduction guidance. [addresses W3, W4]
✅ You now have a real research direction

RO1 defines your baseline experiments. RO2 defines your ablation study. RO3 defines your expected finding. Every experiment in Modules 2–5 is designed to answer these three objectives.

Module 2 · 20 min

Experimental Design & Ablation Study Plan
🧪 What is an ablation study?

Ablation means systematic removal. In ML research, you remove one component at a time — a feature, a layer, a preprocessing step — and observe how performance changes. This reveals what each component actually contributes. A car analogy: remove one wheel at a time to find out which wheel is essential.

Design Principle: Change One Thing at a Time

In a well-controlled experiment, you keep everything the same except the one variable you are testing. This is how you know what caused a performance change.

Your Research Hypothesis

H1 — Feature Importance Hypothesis

Petal features (FS-F) alone will achieve F1-Macro ≥ 0.95 across all classifiers, while sepal features alone (FS-G) will drop below 0.80 — indicating that petal measurements are the primary discriminators in Iris classification.

H2 — Classifier Robustness Hypothesis

Random Forest will show lower standard deviation across CV folds than Decision Tree, demonstrating greater robustness — which single-split evaluation in the anchor paper could not detect.

Module 3 · 15 min

ML Techniques for This Experiment
📌 Everything in this module connects directly to your 21 experiments

You chose DT, RF, and SVM for the experiment matrix in Module 2. This module explains exactly what those algorithms do and why they were chosen. You need Stratified K-Fold CV for RO1, and F1-Macro for RO1–RO3.

The Three Classifiers (Why These Three?)

ClassifierHow It DecidesKey ParameterWhy Include It
Decision Tree (DT) A flowchart of if-then rules learned from data. Splits at each node to best separate classes. max_depth=5 Interpretable baseline. Easy to visualise. The anchor paper used it.
Random Forest (RF) Builds 100+ Decision Trees on random data and feature subsets, then votes. Reduces overfitting. n_estimators=100 Typically more robust than a single tree. Tests H2 (lower variance).
SVM (RBF) Finds the widest possible decision boundary (hyperplane) between classes in high-dimensional space. kernel='rbf' Strong baseline for small, clean datasets. The anchor paper reported it best.

Stratified K-Fold Cross-Validation — The Fix for W1

Instead of one split, we divide the data into 10 equal folds. Each fold takes a turn as the test set while the other 9 train the model. We average the 10 results. Stratified means each fold preserves the original class proportions (50 setosa / 50 versicolor / 50 virginica).

Why F1-Macro, Not Accuracy?

MetricFormula (simplified)Problem with Iris
AccuracyCorrect / TotalTreats all classes equally only when perfectly balanced. Masks per-class failures.
F1-MacroAverage F1 across all 3 classesReports how well each class was handled, then averages — catches systematic failure on one class.

Module 4 · 20 min

Initial Experiments — Baseline (E01–E03)
🎯 Goal: Run E01, E02, E03 only

The baseline uses all 4 features (FS-A) with each classifier. This answers RO1 and gives you the reference point every ablation result will be compared against. Do not skip straight to all 21 experiments.

Baseline Experiment Code (E01–E03)

Python — Baseline: All Features, 3 Classifiers, 10-Fold CV
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.svm import SVC

iris = load_iris()
X_all = iris.data   # Feature Set FS-A: all 4 features
y     = iris.target

# 10-fold stratified CV — same seed for reproducibility
cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=10, shuffle=True, random_state=42)

classifiers = {
    'E01 · Decision Tree': DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=5, random_state=42),
    'E02 · Random Forest': RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=100, random_state=42),
    'E03 · SVM (RBF)'    : SVC(kernel='rbf', random_state=42),
}

print(f"{'Experiment':<25} {'Mean F1-Macro':>14} {'Std':>8}")
print("-" * 50)
for name, clf in classifiers.items():
    scores = cross_val_score(clf, X_all, y, cv=cv, scoring='f1_macro')
    print(f"{name:<25} {scores.mean():>14.4f} {scores.std():>8.4f}")

Module 5 · 30 min

Full Ablation Study in Python + Recording in a Worksheet

Step 5.1 — Run All 21 Experiments Automatically

Instead of running each experiment by hand (21 separate cells), write one loop that iterates over every feature set and classifier combination. This is how professional ML research is coded.

Python — Full Ablation Study (All 21 Experiments)
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.svm import SVC

iris = load_iris()
X = pd.DataFrame(iris.data,
      columns=['sepal_len', 'sepal_wid', 'petal_len', 'petal_wid'])
y = iris.target

# ── Define the 7 feature subsets (from Module 2 experiment plan) ──
FEATURE_SETS = {
    'FS-A All 4'        : ['sepal_len','sepal_wid','petal_len','petal_wid'],
    'FS-B -sepal_len'   : ['sepal_wid','petal_len','petal_wid'],
    'FS-C -sepal_wid'   : ['sepal_len','petal_len','petal_wid'],
    'FS-D -petal_len'   : ['sepal_len','sepal_wid','petal_wid'],
    'FS-E -petal_wid'   : ['sepal_len','sepal_wid','petal_len'],
    'FS-F Petal only'   : ['petal_len','petal_wid'],
    'FS-G Sepal only'   : ['sepal_len','sepal_wid'],
}

# ── Define the 3 classifiers ──
CLASSIFIERS = {
    'Decision Tree': DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=5, random_state=42),
    'Random Forest': RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=100, random_state=42),
    'SVM (RBF)'    : SVC(kernel='rbf', random_state=42),
}

cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=10, shuffle=True, random_state=42)

# ── Run all 21 experiments, collect results ──
exp_id = 1
records = []

for fs_name, features in FEATURE_SETS.items():
    X_sub = X[features].values
    for clf_name, clf in CLASSIFIERS.items():
        scores = cross_val_score(clf, X_sub, y, cv=cv, scoring='f1_macro')
        records.append({
            'Exp_ID'       : f"E{exp_id:02d}",
            'Feature_Set'  : fs_name,
            'Num_Features' : len(features),
            'Classifier'   : clf_name,
            'F1_Macro_Mean': round(scores.mean(), 4),
            'F1_Macro_Std' : round(scores.std(),  4),
            'Meets_RO3'    : 'YES' if scores.mean() >= 0.95 else 'NO',
        })
        exp_id += 1

# ── Build results DataFrame ──
df = pd.DataFrame(records)
print(df.to_string(index=False))

# ── Export to Excel worksheet ──
df.to_excel('ablation_results.xlsx', index=False, sheet_name='Ablation Study')
print("\n✓ Results saved → ablation_results.xlsx")

Part B — How to Read a Paper in 20 Minutes

You do not read a paper like a novel. Use the following order — stop reading if the paper is not relevant at any stage.

1

Abstract (2 minutes)

Is this about my topic? What did they find? If not relevant, stop here.

2

Conclusion (2 minutes)

Jump to the conclusion. What is their main finding? What do they recommend as future work? This is where your gap often hides.

3

Introduction (5 minutes)

What problem did they solve? How do they frame it? What do they claim as their contribution?

4

Methodology & Results (10 minutes)

What data? What models? What metrics? What are the numbers in their results table? Could you replicate this? Note any weaknesses.

5

Record your notes in a reference spreadsheet

Column: Author, Year, Dataset, Methods, Best Result, Weakness/Gap. After 10 papers, you have a literature map.

Part C — Your Paper Structure

A research paper in AI follows a standard structure. Here is what goes in each section, mapped to your experiment.

§1

Introduction

State the problem (W1–W4 from anchor paper). State the gap (single split, no ablation). State your 3 objectives (RO1–RO3). Say what the paper contributes. End with: "This paper is organised as follows…"

§2

Related Work / Literature Review

Summarise 8–12 papers: what methods they used, what datasets, what results. Group them by theme (classifier comparison papers / feature selection papers / Iris-specific papers). End with a gap statement: "However, none of the reviewed studies conducted a feature ablation study with cross-validation."

§3

Methodology

Dataset description. The 7 feature subsets (Table 1 from Module 2). Three classifiers with parameters. CV protocol (Stratified 10-fold, seed=42). Evaluation metric (F1-Macro). Include a pipeline diagram. This section must be detailed enough that someone else can reproduce every result.

§4

Results and Discussion

Present the 21-row results table from your worksheet. Discuss each finding (petal-only meets RO3, sepal-only fails, RF most stable). Compare to the anchor paper. Relate back to each RO. Discuss the limitations of your study.

§5

Conclusion

Summarise findings in 3 sentences (one per RO). State what the petal-only finding means practically. Say what future work could explore (e.g., test on other datasets, add more classifiers, apply Wilcoxon signed-rank test for statistical significance).
